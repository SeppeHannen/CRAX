- Previous works have used TD error to prioritize trajectories for replay, how does that relate to the data attribution we perform in these papers?

For a training record $z_i$, compute the gradient it induces under the actual training objective: $g_i = \nabla_\theta L_{\text{train}}(\theta_k, z_i)$. Then compare it with the gradient of a target $f$ representing some behavior or property we care about: $I_i = g_i^\top \nabla_\theta f$.

The interpretation is:

- the **training gradient** says what this record makes the optimizer do;
- the **target gradient** says what direction would improve the property we care about;
- their dot product measures whether those directions agree.

Positive influence means the record pushes in a helpful direction for that target; negative influence means it pushes against it.

### Local attribution

At optimization round $k$, rollouts $B_k$ are collected using the current policy $\pi_{\theta_k}$. For PPO training, the per-record gradient is

$g_i = \nabla_\theta L_{\mathrm{PPO}}(\theta_k, z_i)$, with $z_i \in B_k$. Two useful targets are considered.

#### Action target

$f_{\text{action}}(\theta) = \log \pi_\theta(a \mid s)$. This asks whether training on $z_i$ makes a particular target action $a$ at state $s$ more or less likely.

#### Return target

$f_R(\theta; \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}) = \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}\left[\log \pi_\theta(a \mid s)\hat A_{\mathrm{ref}}(s,a)\right]$. This is a REINFORCE-style policy-gradient objective with a baseline.

For local attribution, the reference policy is the policy at the start of the round: $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}} = \pi_{\theta_k}$. Because $B_k$ was generated by $\pi_{\theta_k}$, the same rollout buffer can be used to estimate the target gradient. The snapshot approximation is therefore:

$I_i \approx \nabla_\theta L_{\mathrm{PPO}}(\theta_k, z_i)^\top \nabla_1 f(\theta_k, \theta_k)$. The first gradient describes **what this training record makes PPO do**. The second describes **what direction would improve the property we care about**.

The full TracIn formulation can sum these comparisons over intermediate optimization checkpoints, but the practical filtering method uses the initial checkpoint $\theta_k$ for efficiency.

### Why use a REINFORCE-style return target instead of PPO?

The training objective and attribution target have different roles.

PPO clipping controls how aggressively the optimizer updates the policy. The attribution target instead tries to express the direction that improves expected utility under a chosen reference distribution.

Locally, the distinction is small. At $\theta = \theta_{\mathrm{ref}} = \theta_k$, the PPO likelihood ratio is 1, so clipping is inactive and the first-order policy-gradient direction is essentially the same as the unclipped REINFORCE-style direction.

This keeps the target closely aligned with the training objective without making the definition of utility depend on PPO's clipping mechanism.

### Lookahead attribution

Lookahead changes the **distribution used to define the target**, not the point where the TracIn gradients are evaluated.

Local: $\nabla L(\theta_k, z_i)^\top \nabla_1 f(\theta_k, \theta_k)$

Lookahead: $\nabla L(\theta_k, z_i)^\top \nabla_1 f(\theta_k, \theta_{k+H})$

Both gradients are evaluated at $\theta_k$.

The future policy $\pi_{\theta_{k+H}}$ is used to generate the target rollout distribution and future advantage estimates. The question becomes:

> Did this old training record push the policy in a direction that would have been useful according to the behavior and utility observed under a later policy?

A useful shorthand is:

- local target: $B_k, \hat A_k$
- lookahead target: $B_{k+H}, \hat A_{k+H}$

A separate method, SGD-influence, does use a target gradient at the future checkpoint and propagates it backward through a linearized approximation of the intervening optimization dynamics. That should not be confused with the simpler TracIn picture above.

### Attribution for generalization

The same construction can be applied to held-out evaluation environments.

At checkpoint $\theta_k$, roll out the current policy on an evaluation-environment distribution that is not used for training, producing $B_{\mathrm{eval},k}$.

Define an evaluation target such as

$f_{\mathrm{eval}}(\theta; \theta_k) = \mathbb{E}_{B_{\mathrm{eval},k}}\left[\log \pi_\theta(a \mid s)\hat A_{\mathrm{eval}}(s,a)\right]$. Then:

$I_i^{\mathrm{gen}} = \nabla L_{\mathrm{train}}(\theta_k, z_i)^\top \nabla_1 f_{\mathrm{eval}}(\theta_k, \theta_k)$. This asks:

> Does this training record move the policy in a direction that improves behavior on held-out environments?

The evaluation buffer does **not** need to be globally fixed. A fresh on-policy evaluation buffer can be collected at each checkpoint; it only needs to be treated as fixed while computing the target gradient.

A lookahead version is also possible by evaluating old training records against held-out rollouts from a future policy.

### Extension to constrained RL

For constrained RL, a natural training objective is PPO-Lagrangian: $L_{\mathrm{train}} = L_{\mathrm{PPOLag}} \approx L_R^{\mathrm{PPO}} - \lambda L_C^{\mathrm{PPO}}$

with signs depending on the cost convention.

The per-record training gradient becomes $g_i^{\mathrm{train}} = \nabla_\theta L_{\mathrm{PPOLag}}(\theta_k, z_i)$.

The attribution targets can then be separated according to what we want to understand.

#### Reward target

$f_R(\theta; \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}) = \mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}\left[\log \pi_\theta(a \mid s)\hat A_R(s,a)\right]$

#### Cost target

$f_C(\theta; \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}) = \mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}\left[\log \pi_\theta(a \mid s)\hat A_C(s,a)\right]$

#### Combined Lagrangian target

$f_{\mathrm{Lag}} = f_R - \lambda f_C$. This gives separate attribution scores such as $I_i^R = (g_i^{\mathrm{train}})^\top \nabla f_R$ and $I_i^C = (g_i^{\mathrm{train}})^\top \nabla f_C$.

Keeping reward and cost attribution separate is useful because a single record may:

- help reward and help safety;
- help reward but hurt safety;
- hurt reward but help safety;
- hurt both.

A combined Lagrangian attribution score can still be used when we specifically want to know whether a record helped the current constrained objective.

The same reward and cost targets can be evaluated locally, with lookahead, or on a held-out evaluation distribution.

### Actor vs. critic filtering

A record that is harmful for the actor is not necessarily harmful for the critic.

For example, a transition may induce a poor actor update because its estimated advantage is inaccurate, while still providing useful information for fitting the value function.

A useful extension is therefore to distinguish $I_i^{\mathrm{actor}}$ and $I_i^{\mathrm{critic}}$, and potentially use

$B_{\mathrm{actor}} = B_{\mathrm{filtered}}$, $B_{\mathrm{critic}} = B$. Whether this helps is an empirical question, especially when actor and critic share parameters or when critic targets themselves are noisy.

### Practical mental model

The framework can be summarized as two questions:

> **What did this data point make training do?**

and

> **What direction would improve the property I care about?**

The attribution score measures how well those directions align.

Local, lookahead, generalization, reward, and safety attribution mainly differ in **how the target gradient is defined and which rollout distribution is used to estimate it**.

#### Related work and contribution

They refer to a [survey of data attribution](https://hal.science/hal-05230469) by (Junwei Deng 2025) and to a survey on [explainable reinforcement learning](https://dl.acm.org/doi/full/10.1145/3616864) by (Stephanie Milani 2024) and to a survey on [explainable deep reinforcement learning](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.06869) by (Zelei Cheng 2025).

In their related work section they mention that early work focuses on **feature-level explainability** (highlighting observation space regions that most influence an agent's decision), **policy-level explainability** (approximating learned policies with human-interpretable rules / designing transparent architectures / dissecting reward functions to clarify action choices), and more recently **trajectory explainability** (how entire training trajectories shape behavior).

The methods that look at *critical states* offer a finer-grained view of decision making. Several methods address the offline RL setting. The online RL methods that investigate states require modifying the training pipeline by augmenting states or training auxiliary mask networks. **These methods rely on the policy being sufficiently developed, which limits applicability in complex environments when agents are still learning**.

They develop a local attribution framework that uses model checkpoints and records in the recent training buffer.

They design two target functions:

1. agent action
2. cumulative return

and they measure the contribution of each training record to this target through gradient similarity between training loss and these targets.

They take their learnings and apply them to the training algorithm to reduce sample complexity, speed up training, and achieve higher returns. They call it **iterative influence-based filtering (IFF)**.

#### Definition of Online RL

The environment $\mathcal{E}$ is modeled as a Markov Decision Process (MDP) defined by the tuple $(\mathcal{S}, \mathcal{A}, P, R, \gamma, d_0)$. Online RL typically proceeds in alternating training rounds of data collection and model training. In round $k$, the data collection phase involves the agent executing the current policy $\pi_{\theta^{(k)}}$, sampling experiences over multiple episodes to accumulate $n$ transition records in a rollout buffer $B^{(k)}$. Each record contains the raw transition $(s_t, a_t, r_t)$ and several computed quantities (including the action log probability $\log \pi_{\theta^{(k)}}$, estimated value $v_t$, and advantage estimate $\hat{A}_t$). Model parameters are then updated iteratively, starting from $\theta_0^{(k)} = \theta^{(k)}$, at optimization step $j$ training on the mini-batch $\mathcal{B}_j^{(k)}$ drawn from $B^{(k)}$ updates parameters from $\theta_j^{(k)}$ to $\theta_{j+1}^{(k)}$.

Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) is a policy gradient method for online RL that optimizes a clipped surrogate function which is typically combined with a value function loss and an entropy bonus during optimization, defined as:

$\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{(s,a)\sim\mathcal{B}_{j}^{(k)}}\left[ \min \left( \frac{\pi_\theta(a \mid s)}{\pi_{\theta^{(k)}}(a \mid s)}\hat{A}(s,a), \text{clip}\left( \frac{\pi_\theta(a \mid s)}{\pi_{\theta^{(k)}}(a \mid s)}, 1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon \right)\hat{A}(s,a) \right) \right]$, where $\epsilon$ is a hyperparameter that limits policy changes between rounds and promotes stable learning.

#### Data attribution

They mention that common data attribution techniques in machine learning are:

- influence functions,
- Data Shapley,
- SGD-influence,
- TracIn,
- TRAK.

They focus on [TracIn](https://arxiv.org/abs/2002.08484) (by Garima Pruthi from Google) because of its conceptual simplicity, relative efficiency and widespread use in recent works.

**TracIn** measures the cumulative change in a *target function* $f(\theta)$ resulting from the optimization steps involving a specific training sample $z_i$. Consider training a model parameterized by $\theta$ on a training set $\{z_i\}_{i=1}^n$ by minimizing the empirical loss $\sum_{i=1}^n l(\theta, z_i)$ using stochastic gradient descent (SGD). At step $j$, with parameters $\theta_j$, learning rate $\eta_j$, and mini-batch $\mathcal{B}_j$, a first-order Taylor expansion of $f(\theta)$ around $\theta_j$ gives:

$f(\theta_j) - f(\theta_{j+1}) \approx \nabla_\theta f(\theta_j) \cdot (\theta_j - \theta_{j+1})=\eta_j \sum_{i\in \mathcal{B}_j} \nabla_\theta f(\theta_j) \cdot \nabla_\theta l(\theta_j, z_i)$. Accumulating these contributions over the relevant training iterations $j$ (the training iterations that include the data point $z_i$) yields the TracIn score for $z_i$:

$\text{TracIn}(z_i) = \sum_{j: z_i\in \mathcal{B}_j}\eta_j\nabla_\theta f(\theta_j) \cdot \nabla_\theta l(\theta_j, z_i)$.

#### Framework

They state that current data attribution methods include *retraining-based* and *gradient-based* methods. The gradient-based methods include both *static* and *dynamic* methods. The *static* methods assume model optimization over a fixed dataset which is not the case for RL as the dataset changes every iteration. The *dynamic* methods (including TracIn) can deal with dataset changes per iteration but do not account for the fact that the model determines what the next data is that gets trained on. Therefore these methods do not identify the contribution of a datapoint $z_i$ via the influence it has on the future data that the model is trained on, which they call *data-model dependency*. As a result, the methods may deviate significantly from the true influence they seek to measure. They mention that quantifying influences through data-model dependency is *challenging because sampling is stochastic and non-differentiable*.

The authors mention that Online RL involves a local policy optimization structure, i.e. round $k$ optimized on a fixed buffer $B^{(k)}$ of on-policy data. Their framework operates at this level, examining how records in $B^{(k)}$ contribute to the updates from $\theta^{(k)}$ to $\theta^{(k+1)}$. This circumvents the challenges in tracing influence through the non-differentiable dependencies across the training history.

Their **entity of attribution** is an individual training record in the rollout buffer, $z_i = (s_i, a_i, r_i, \log \pi_i, v_i, \hat{A}_i)$, collected from the environment using the current policy $\theta^{(k)}$.

Their framework computes attribution from training points to two targets: **agent action** and **cumulative return**.

Agent action is the agent's decision to take action $a$ in state $s$, $f^{\text{action}}(\theta) := \log \pi_\theta(a \mid s)$.

Cumulative return is ideally $J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim \pi_\theta}[R(\tau)]$ where $R(\tau)=\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}r_t$ and trajectories $\tau$ are sampled by executing $\pi_\theta$. Problem 1: there is no fixed validation set in online RL. Problem 2: the raw returns $R(\tau)$ exhibit high variance leading to noisy influence estimates. To address these challenges they introduce a stable surrogate objective based on a reference policy $\pi^{\text{ref}}$ and advantage estimates $\hat{A}^{\text{ref}}$:

$f^{\text{return}}(\theta):= \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim \pi^{\text{ref}}, (s,a)\sim\tau} \left[ \log \pi_\theta(a \mid s) \hat{A}^{\text{ref}}(s,a)\right]$. This target function is structurally equivalent to the objective of REINFORCE with a baseline (Sutton and Barto, 2018, Section 13.4). By sampling from $\pi^{\text{ref}}$ they obtain a fixed evaluation distribution; using advantage estimates significantly reduces variance compared to raw returns. Maximizing $f^{\text{return}}$ encourages increasing the probability of better-than-average actions and decreasing worse-than-average ones, capturing the essence of improving expected return while being tractable.

They set the reference policy $\pi^{\text{ref}} = \pi_{\theta^{(k)}}$ for attribution round $k$, i.e. the policy snapshot at the beginning of the round. This is a key design choice of the contextual framework, which enables them to ask: For the agent at its current stage of training, which experiences will be most helpful or harmful for the next update? Unlike a fixed, off-distribution reference that may provide misleading signals due to mismatch with the agent's current state, the dynamic reference evolves with training, providing a stable and relevant basis for meaningful evaluation and attribution. Furthermore, since the training rollout buffer $B^{(k)}$ is collected under $\pi_{\theta^{(k)}}$, they can directly use it as the validation dataset.

They adapt TracIn to the Online RL setting for their **method of attribution**. For record $z_i$ in the rollout buffer $B^{(k)}$ they compute its *influence score* by summing over the optimization steps $j$ within round $k$:

$I_i := \sum_{j: z_i \in \mathcal{B}_j^{(k)}} \left\langle \nabla_\theta f\left(\theta_j^{(k)}\right), \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}^{\text{PPO}}\left(\theta_j^{(k)}, z_i\right) \right\rangle, \text{where } f \in \{f^{\text{action}}, f^{\text{return}}\}$. They also discuss several design choices which substantially reduce the computational and storage costs of the vanilla TracIn.

The influence scores can be interpreted as follows. Records with positive influence benefit behavior formation or learning, whereas those with negative influence harm it. They refer to records with the most positive influence as top records and those with the most negative influence as bottom records.

## Conclusion

For a data point $z_i$, the influence score compares the PPO update induced by $z_i$ with the gradient of a chosen target. They do mention that the $f^{\text{return}}$ target relies on potentially inaccurate advantage estimates but that the aggregated signal is reasonably robust because of its alignment with the PPO objective. They also ran an experiment in section 4.3 that showed that the return target improves the return received when performing a 1 round counterfactual throughout virtually all rounds in the training process.

### Action influence target

The target is $f^{\text{action}}(\theta)=\log \pi_\theta(a\mid s)$.

The influence tells us whether training on $z_i$ makes a particular queried action $a$ in state $s$ more or less likely.

- **Positive influence:** $z_i$ reinforces the behavior $(s,a)$.
- **Negative influence:** $z_i$ suppresses the behavior $(s,a)$.
- **Near zero:** $z_i$ has little effect on that behavior.

> **Action influence asks:** Which data points helped teach or suppress this particular behavior?

### Return influence target

The gradient of the return target represents the aggregate direction that should locally improve return, based on the state-action pairs and advantages generated by the reference policy.

In the paper, the reference policy is the current policy, so this becomes the overall return-improving direction suggested by the current rollout batch.

The return influence tells us whether the PPO update induced by $z_i$ aligns with that direction.

- **Positive influence:** $z_i$ pushes the policy in a direction aligned with improving return.
- **Negative influence:** $z_i$ pushes against that direction.
- **Near zero:** $z_i$ contributes little to the return-improving direction.

> **Return influence asks:** Which data points helped or hurt the local return-improving update?

## Extension to Safe / Constrained RL (ours)

**The capability: for any training record, say whether it made the policy safer or more dangerous.**

Nobody can currently do this. Safe-RL curriculum papers show that a curriculum improved safety; none can point at which experiences did it. With per-record safety scores you can map where reward and safety conflict at the level of individual states, aggregate per environment to ask which environments a curriculum should be sampling and when, and filter or reweight harmful records during training. The first two are the thesis; filtering is parked below.

The mechanism is a cost target alongside Hu et al.'s return target — same form, cost advantage instead of reward advantage:

$\hat f^{\text{cost}} = \tfrac{1}{n}\sum_m \log\pi_\theta(a_m|s_m)\hat A_m^{C}$. No sign flip; a safety score is $-I^{\text{cost}}$ applied at reporting. The loss inside the influence score stays the Lagrangian one PPOLag actually applies — TracIn needs the loss that moved the parameters, not one matched to the target.

Measure both targets rather than the Lagrangian score alone. Since $\lambda$ is constant within a round, $I^{\text{lag}} = (I^{\text{return}} - \lambda I^{\text{cost}})/(1+\lambda)$, so the pair recovers the single score at any $\lambda$ but not the reverse. A record with $I^{\text{lag}} \approx 0$ may be inert, or may help reward and harm safety with the two cancelling at the current $\lambda$.

| $I^{\text{return}}$ | $I^{\text{cost}}$ | reading |
|---|---|---|
| + | − | good on both |
| − | + | bad on both |
| + | + | **conflict case** — reward at the expense of safety |
| − | − | safety at the expense of reward |

#### Experiment 1: does the cost score predict anything?

Do this first. If it works, the capability is real and everything downstream is unblocked. If it fails, you know in a month, and the reason is diagnostic: either $\hat A^C$ carries no signal, or the estimator doesn't transfer to sparse-cost settings.

At a fixed round, remove ~50 random subsets from the buffer, re-run that round's optimization from the same snapshot, and correlate predicted $\sum I^{\text{cost}}$ against the measured change in episodic cost. Repeat at ~10 checkpoints across training. Use `safe_goal_point` — hazards give a real reward/safety conflict, whereas in `safe_velocity` cost and reward are nearly collinear.

Include an advantage-only control and don't skip it. Since $I_i = \hat A_i^{\text{lag}} \times \text{(gradient alignment)}$, ranking by advantage alone is a strong baseline rather than a strawman. If influence doesn't beat it, you've built an expensive advantage detector.

The likely real finding is that attribution quality varies over training: poor early while the cost critic is unfit, best mid-training while $\lambda$ binds, degenerate late once the policy is feasible and $\hat A^C \approx 0$. That curve is itself a result.

Scope the claim honestly. Success means the score predicts *one-round* changes in cost, not that a record made the final policy safe. The cross-round step is separate and unbuilt.

Before any of this, check the cost critic's explained variance on held-out rollouts and the base rate of the cost signal. Every score is built on $\hat A^C$, so if the critic is poorly fit — plausible with rare binary hazard contact — everything is noise. One afternoon, and it decides whether the project as scoped is viable.

#### Experiment 2: map where reward and safety conflict

Once Experiment 1 holds, plot the states of conflict-quadrant records. Expect a band at hazard boundaries, the shortcut states, and track how it moves across rounds. Cheap, descriptive, nobody has it — and it shows *which situations* the agent must be taught, not just that some curriculum worked.

## Extension to curriculum learning (ours)

Each record traces back to the environment instance that produced it, so scores can be aggregated per environment to ask which environments produced useful data. Aggregate within a round only — this avoids assuming that scores compose across rounds, which Hu et al. don't establish.

#### Experiment 3: measure when each environment started mattering

For a curriculum already known to work, show when each environment began producing high-influence cost data, and whether the curriculum shifted sampling toward it around then. That would be the first attribution-based account of *why* a safe-RL curriculum works.

Two things will break it. The target must not come from the curriculum's own sampling, or a heavily-sampled environment looks well-aligned by construction — use a separate batch collected under the same policy but sampled uniformly, or over held-out environments, which turns the score into a measure of improvement on the test distribution. And the measure is a diagnostic, not an objective: scores are local to the current policy, so an environment scoring low now may be exactly what makes a harder one learnable later, and maximizing this greedily would produce a bad curriculum.

Practically, rank or normalize within a round, since magnitudes drift with gradient norms, $\lambda$, and advantage normalization. This also needs a curriculum that samples a mixture of environments per round; a sequential difficulty schedule gives no within-round comparison, so vary something that does change within a round, such as hazard layout.

#### Parked

**Filtering (IFF port).** Hu et al.'s downstream use, not the thesis question. The records you'd drop ($I^{\text{cost}}>0$, $I^{\text{return}}\leq 0$) are mostly the actual violations — the cost critic's only positive examples under sparse binary cost. Filtering removes them from the critic update too, corrupting $\hat A^C$ and all later attribution. The fix is to drop from the policy update but keep in the critic update. A deeper worry survives that fix: filtering on agreement with the batch shrinks spread around a direction estimated from the same data, which is fine for reward where disagreement is mostly noise, but questionable for cost where violations are rare and an opposing record is more likely genuine signal. The fix protects the critic, not the policy.

**Attribute the critic itself** (target $V_C^\phi(s)$, loss $\mathcal{L}_{\text{critic}}$): which records taught the agent to *recognise* danger rather than avoid it.

**Refs to find.** Gradient-agreement filtering has precedents outside data attribution (gradient surgery, agreement-based weighting). The attribution framing, especially the action target, is the more novel part of the paper.

# TracIn

- Setting: supervised learning, SGD
- Training data studied: examples
- Outcome attributed to: test loss

The ideal TracIn has the property that the sum of the influences of all training examples on a fixed test point $z'$ is exactly the total reduction in loss on $z'$ in the training process:

$\sum_{i=1}^n \text{TracInIdeal}(z_i, z')=l(w_0, z') - l(w_T, z')$. The authors call out that practical gradient descent algorithms use minibatches and that we cannot extend the definition of idealized influence to this setting because there is no obvious way to redistribute the loss change across members of the minibatch.

In their minibatch version of TracIn they define it as:

$\text{TracIn}(z, z') = \frac{1}{b}\sum_{t: z\in B_t} \eta_t \nabla l(w_t, z') \cdot \nabla l(w_t, z)$, where the factor $\frac{1}{b}$ comes from the minibatch gradient being an average, so $z$ contributes only $-\frac{\eta_t}{b}\nabla \ell(w_t, z)$ to the update.

# Non-Local Data Attribution for On-Policy RL

- Setting: online RL

Their **entity of attribution** is now the *occurrence* $z_{i,j}$, the $j$-th use of a record during optimization. With ten epochs per buffer, each record contributes ten occurrences.

**Ground truth.** They define **replay-LOO**: fix the buffers $B_i,\dots,B_{i+H-1}$, replay the identical curriculum with $z_{i,j}$ removed, and measure the change in the target at the end of the horizon: $\Delta^{\text{replay}}_{i,H}(z_{i,j}) := f\left(\theta^{\backslash(i,j),\text{replay}}_{i+H}, \pi_{\theta_{i+H}}\right) - f\left(\theta_{i+H}, \pi_{\theta_{i+H}}\right)$. The sign is "without minus with", so a positive value means the occurrence was harmful. Both terms use the original run's $\pi_{\theta_{i+H}}$ as reference, so the evaluation distribution is held fixed. Because the buffers are frozen, an occurrence can only exert influence through the parameters it updated, never through the future data it would have caused the agent to collect. They define *recollection-LOO* for that second channel but leave it to future work.

The target function is unchanged, $f(\theta,\pi^{\text{ref}}) = \mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi^{\text{ref}}}[\log\pi_\theta(a\mid s)\hat A^{\text{ref}}(s,a)]$. Only the choice of $\pi^{\text{ref}}$ changes. Both experiments correlate a TracIn score against replay-LOO; the only thing that differs is which reference policy the score's target uses.

### Why the lookahead target should correlate better

Expand the ground truth to first order. Removing $z_{i,j}$ perturbs the parameters by roughly $-\eta\nabla_\theta\ell(\theta_i;z_{i,j})$, and this perturbation $\delta\theta$ propagates to the end of the horizon. The resulting change in the target is then $\Delta^{\text{replay}} \approx \left\langle \nabla_\theta f\left(\theta_{i+H}, \pi_{\theta_{i+H}}\right),\ \delta\theta \right\rangle$. So the ground truth is *itself* an inner product against $\nabla_\theta f(\cdot,\pi_{\theta_{i+H}})$. The lookahead score projects the same perturbation onto that same vector, differing only in where it is evaluated ($\theta_i$ rather than $\theta_{i+H}$) and in ignoring how $\delta\theta$ is reshaped along the way. The local score projects onto a *different* vector, $\nabla_\theta f(\cdot,\pi_{\theta_i})$.

The correlation gap is therefore essentially the angle between the return-improving directions under $\pi_{\theta_i}$ and under $\pi_{\theta_{i+H}}$. That angle grows as the policy moves, which explains why local correlation degrades for occurrences from earlier rollouts.

### Local attribution

$\pi^{\text{ref}} = \pi_{\theta_i}$ recovers the first paper's target exactly. Spearman 0.53 against replay-LOO at $\theta_{i+5}$, degrading for earlier rollouts.

### Lookahead evaluation

$\pi^{\text{ref}} = \pi_{\theta_{i+H}}$. The score is still an inner product between two gradient vectors:

- **First vector:** the parameter update induced by training on $z_{i,j}$ (unchanged).
- **Second vector:** the direction increasing log-probability of positive-advantage actions, where both the states and the advantage estimates come from the *future* policy and its critic. The current parameters enter only through $\log\pi_\theta(a\mid s)$ on those future pairs.

TracIn evaluates the inner product at $\theta_i$ only; SGDI evaluates $\nabla f$ at $\theta_{i+H}$ and propagates the perturbation through $\prod_s(I-\eta_s H_s)$. Spearman 0.9812 and 0.9857. The small gap says the Hessian correction adds little, i.e. the replay dynamics over five rollouts are close to linear.

> **Lookahead attribution asks:** Which occurrences pushed the policy along the return-improving direction defined on future rollouts?

Given the derivation above, the 0.98 is less surprising than it first looks: the estimator and the ground truth are built from the same $f$, so the result mainly confirms that the first-order approximation holds over this horizon. Whether $f$ itself tracks true return is argued only indirectly, through the LIF curves.

### LIF

Train $H$ rounds normally while caching buffers and per-step gradients, score every occurrence, return to $\theta_i$ and replay the curriculum with the bottom $p$ fraction discarded. Outperforms IIF and standard PPO. The ablation is the key result: replacing the lookahead target with the local one collapses performance back to IIF, so the gain comes from the target rather than the filtering. Replaying a filtered curriculum is inherently off-policy: the cached transitions were collected by the original policies, but update parameters that drift further from them at each replayed step. They overwrite each occurrence's stored $\log\pi_{i,j}$ with its value under the current checkpoint $\theta'_{i+k}$, which re-anchors the PPO ratio at 1. But the buffer's states and advantages still belong to the original policy, so the update optimizes a distribution the policy is no longer on. They report the return curve plateauing at later rollouts within a horizon, and it resets whenever a new horizon collects fresh data.

## Possible extension to constrained RL (ours)

Everything below is relative to Eq. (1), (2), (4) and Algorithm 1. Training is PPOLag, so the loss in Eq. (4) is $\ell^{\text{lag}} = \frac{1}{1+\lambda}(\ell - \lambda \ell^{C})$ and each occurrence also carries a cost advantage $\hat A^{C}_{i,j}$.

#### Step 0: re-establish replay-LOO for cost

Nothing in Sec. 2.3 transfers automatically. The 0.98 was measured with $f^{\text{return}}$; the changes below all assume the same holds with $f^{\text{cost}}$, which is an empirical question. Eq. (2) instantiated on cost:

$\Delta^{\text{replay},C}_{i,H}(z_{i,j}) := f^{\text{cost}}\!\left( \theta^{\backslash(i,j),\text{replay}}_{i+H}, \pi^{\text{ref}} \right) - f^{\text{cost}}\!\left( \theta_{i+H}, \pi^{\text{ref}} \right)$,

with the same $\pi^{\text{ref}}$ (and, under Change 2, the same frozen $\hat P$) in both terms, so the evaluation distribution is held fixed. Same protocol as Sec. 2.2–2.3: 1% of occurrences, correlate against local and lookahead $I^{\text{cost}}$.

Two reasons it may correlate worse than the return case. The first-order argument behind the 0.98 assumes $\delta\theta$ propagates near-linearly over the horizon, which is fine; but it also needs the target gradient to be well-estimated, and $\hat A^{C}$ from a critic fit on rare events is noisier than $\hat A$. And the training gradient is $\ell^{\text{lag}}$ while the target is pure cost, so the two are less aligned than in the paper where loss and target are both about return — the paper's own robustness argument for $f^{\text{return}}$ (Hu et al. Sec. 4.3: it works because it aligns with the PPO objective) does not apply.

Include the advantage-only control from Experiment 1. $I^{\text{cost}} = \hat A^{\text{lag}}_{i,j} \times (\text{gradient alignment})$, so ranking by cost advantage alone is a real baseline.

#### Change 1: a cost target

In Eq. (1), replace $\hat A^{\text{ref}}$ with $\hat A^{C,\text{ref}}$. Call it $f^{\text{cost}}$; the original is $f^{\text{return}}$. Eq. (4) is unchanged apart from $\ell \to \ell^{\text{lag}}$.

The score then measures whether optimizing on $z_{i,j}$ under the PPOLag objective moves the policy in a direction that changes safety for the reference policy on its distribution — same question as $I^{\text{return}}$, with the cost critic in place of the reward critic. Reference policy is $\pi_{\theta_{i+H}}$ by default, as in Eq. (4); Change 2 argues that for cost it shouldn't be.

One catch: $f^{\text{cost}}$ inherits Eq. (1)'s sign, so its gradient points toward *raising* cost. Alignment means the occurrence made things worse. Report $-I^{\text{cost}}$. (Defining the target with $-\hat A^{C}$ would fix the sign but break $I^{\text{lag}} = (I^{\text{return}} - \lambda I^{\text{cost}})/(1+\lambda)$, which is why measuring both scores recovers the Lagrangian one at any $\lambda$ and not the reverse.)

#### Change 2: take the expectation over chosen scenarios

For the cost target, take Eq. (1)'s expectation over a probe buffer $\hat P$ — rollouts of $\pi_{\theta_{i+H}}$ in fixed held-out hazard layouts — instead of over $B_{i+H-1}$. Critic unchanged. In Algorithm 1 this is line 7 with $\hat P$ in place of $B_{i+H-1}$.

The score then measures whether $z_{i,j}$ improves safety *in the scenarios you chose to care about*, rather than in whatever states the agent currently wanders into. That is the question a safety claim is actually making.

Secondary reason, weaker than it first looks. Return is being maximized, but a constraint has a fixed point training drives the policy toward, so once it is satisfied the thing you are attributing stops being produced: with sparse hazard-contact cost, a safer $\pi_{\theta_{i+H}}$ gives buffers with few contacts and $\hat A^{C} \approx 0$ almost everywhere. Held-out hazards keep supplying contacts. But PPOLag targets a threshold, not zero, so the policy sits at the boundary and still violates — the effect is partial, and `normalize_advantage=true` rescales what is left.

Cost: $\hat P$ needs real rollouts, since $\hat A^{C}$ needs observed cost returns.

Pre-check: track the cost base rate and the cost critic's explained variance on $B_{i+H-1}$ versus $\hat P$ across training. Gradient norm alone is misleading under advantage normalization.

#### Change 3: sum per environment over the horizon

$I^{\text{cost}}_e = \sum_{z_{i,j} \text{ from } e} I^{\text{cost}}(z_{i,j})$ over all $H$ rollouts, rank-normalized within rollout.

This measures which environments produced data that improved safety over the horizon, rather than in a single update. Sec. 2.3's 0.98 is what licenses summing across rollouts at all; Hu et al. never established that.

Sweep $H \in \{1,2,5\}$ on a curriculum known to work: larger $H$ credits environments whose payoff arrives later, which is the standard objection to attribution-driven curricula. Needs environments mixed within a rollout.

# Data Attribution in Adaptive Learning

### Core idea

In online RL, changing the importance of one training occurrence can affect the final policy in two ways. It can directly change the parameter updates that follow, but it can also change the policy itself and therefore change which future data gets collected. A complete notion of influence should account for both effects: **how the occurrence changed learning on later data, and how it changed the later data itself**.

This matters for TracIn and Lookahead TracIn because they naturally operate on a realized training trajectory. They estimate what happens when a training occurrence is changed while the later rollout data is effectively kept fixed. This is useful, but it answers a narrower question than: **What would actually have happened if this occurrence had been downweighted and the agent had then continued interacting with the environment?** The distinction can be large enough that the two answers even have opposite signs.

### When the effect on future data is tractable

An important special case is when the learner can affect the environment only through its **actions**. Changing the policy may change which actions are selected, which then changes the states and rewards encountered, but the environment does not otherwise respond directly to the learner's parameters. This is close to the usual RL picture where the environment reacts to $s$ and $a$, not to the neural-network weights that produced $a$.

In this setting, for a future trajectory that was actually observed, we can calculate how much more or less likely that trajectory would have been under the perturbed policy:

$\prod_s \frac{\pi_{\text{perturbed}}(a_s\mid s_s)} {\pi_{\text{original}}(a_s\mid s_s)}$. This ratio can be used as an **importance weight**. Futures that become more likely under the perturbed policy receive more weight, while futures that become less likely receive less weight. In this way, logged trajectories can be reweighted to approximate the distribution of future experience that would have arisen after changing the earlier training occurrence.

### Relevance for TracIn in constrained RL

For my constrained-RL work, this suggests that TracIn or Lookahead TracIn should initially be viewed as measuring **influence conditioned on the observed future training data**. A possible extension would be to combine this gradient-based influence with a correction based on policy likelihood ratios, so that the score also reflects how an occurrence changed the probability of later trajectories.

Such an extension would move the attribution question closer to: **How did this training occurrence affect the eventual reward or safety outcome, including through the future experiences it caused the agent to collect?**

A key technical requirement is **support / overlap**: every future action or trajectory that can occur under the perturbed policy must also have had non-zero probability under the original policy. Otherwise, the perturbed policy may visit parts of the trajectory space that are completely absent from the logged data, and no likelihood-ratio reweighting can recover them. In practice, this means the method is most plausible for relatively small policy perturbations and policies that retain sufficient exploration, rather than near-deterministic policies where some relevant actions have zero or negligible probability.

# Algorithm-Relative Trajectory Valuation

The authors write that data attribution tooling for machine learning such as **Influence Functions** and **Shapley values** offer principled frameworks for quantifying marginal performance contributions, however, policy-gradient learning introduces algorithm-data interactions absent in i.i.d. settings.

**Persistence of Excitation (PE)** measures how well a trajectory explores all relevant state/action directions. Under a fixed total trajectory energy, high PE means that exploration is spread relatively evenly across directions rather than being concentrated in only a few.

The paper shows that **PE is not an algorithm-independent measure of trajectory value**. Under vanilla REINFORCE, low-PE trajectories can appear valuable because they induce higher-variance gradients, and that noise can help optimization escape saddle regions. Whitening removes this variance advantage, after which high-PE trajectories become more valuable.

The important point is therefore not that our attribution method is optimizer-dependent, but that **a trajectory property such as PE cannot be treated as a universal proxy for trajectory value**. How useful that property is depends on how the learning algorithm turns the trajectory into updates.

# Understanding Data Influence in Reinforcement Finetuning

The paper mentions the seminal work that applies **influence functions** to machine learning, [*Understanding Black-box Predictions via Influence Functions*](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1703.04730). The method locally approximates the empirical loss around the trained parameters $\hat\theta$ using its Hessian $H$. It then uses $H^{-1}$ together with the gradient of a particular training example $z_i$ to approximate how the local optimum—and therefore the model parameters—would move if that example were removed from the training set. The resulting estimated parameter change can then be projected onto the gradient of a target function, such as the loss on a test example, to approximate how removing $z_i$ would affect that target.

Influence functions and TracIn attribute influence in fundamentally different ways:

- **TracIn is trajectory-based:** it asks how much a training point actually helped or hurt a target point through the parameter updates made during training. It accumulates gradient alignment across checkpoints.
- **Influence functions are counterfactual and endpoint-based:** they ask how the final trained solution would move if a training point were slightly upweighted or removed. They estimate that movement using the inverse Hessian, which accounts for the local curvature of the loss around the final optimum.

Intuitively: **TracIn follows what happened during training; influence functions approximate what would happen if you perturbed the training set and re-optimized.**

They mention several other works that build on this work to make it more efficient or more applicable.

The paper explains GRPO which uses sampling to avoid the need for a value network which makes sense when fine-tuning LLMs.

## RFT-Inf

The ideal quantity they want for a training sample $z_i$ is its leave-one-out effect on the reward of the trained model:

$D(z_i)=J(\theta_T)-J(\theta_T^{-i})$,

where $\theta_T$ is the endpoint obtained by training on the full dataset and $\theta_T^{-i}$ is the endpoint that would be obtained by rerunning training without $z_i$.

Computing this exactly would require retraining once for every sample.

### From the ideal quantity to the gradient-alignment approximation

They decompose the final reward difference into changes accumulated over the training trajectory and apply first-order Taylor approximations to those per-step changes.

This leads to a score of the form:

$\hat D(z_i) \approx \sum_t \eta_t \left\langle \nabla_\theta L(\theta_t,z_i), \nabla_\theta L_{\mathrm{train}}(\theta_t) \right\rangle$,

up to the constant factors used in the paper, where $\nabla_\theta L_{\mathrm{train}}(\theta_t) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_j \nabla_\theta L(\theta_t,z_j)$.

So each term asks:

> Does the gradient induced by $z_i$ align with the overall training direction at checkpoint $t$?

These local gradient alignments are then summed over the trajectory.

The derivation is approximate: it relies on first-order Taylor expansions and on approximating how removing one sample changes the aggregate gradient. The paper also gives an error bound that grows with the length of the trajectory.

### What they do in practice

They do not compute this score throughout the full expensive RFT run.

Instead they:
1. run a short surrogate RFT on the full dataset;
2. save a small number of checkpoints;
3. at each checkpoint compute $\nabla_\theta L(\theta_t,z_i)$ for every training sample;
4. compute the overall training gradient $\nabla_\theta L_{\mathrm{train}}(\theta_t)$;
5. sum the gradient alignments across the surrogate checkpoints;
6. rank the training samples by this score;
7. keep only the highest-scoring subset and perform the actual RFT on that subset.

So there are three levels:

true leave-one-out reward effect → trajectory-wide first-order approximation → short-surrogate checkpoint estimate.

The theory directly motivates the middle step. Using a short surrogate run to predict which samples will remain useful during the later full RFT run is ultimately an empirical proxy.

### Relation to TracIn

In the notation already used in these notes, TracIn is

$\operatorname{TracIn}(z_i) = \sum_t \eta_t \left\langle \nabla_\theta f(\theta_t), \nabla_\theta L(\theta_t,z_i) \right\rangle$.

RFT-Inf has almost exactly the same computational form, except that the target direction is chosen to be the overall RFT training gradient:

$\nabla_\theta f(\theta_t) \quad\longleftrightarrow\quad \nabla_\theta L_{\mathrm{train}}(\theta_t)$.

So computationally, RFT-Inf is very close to TracIn.

The main difference is the interpretation: TracIn starts from cumulative influence on a chosen target $f$, while RFT-Inf starts from a leave-one-out reward difference and derives a TracIn-like gradient-alignment sum as an approximation to it.

### Relation to the local RL return target

The local RL attribution paper uses

$I_i = \sum_{t\text{ in round }k} \left\langle \nabla_\theta f^{\mathrm{return}}(\theta_t), \nabla_\theta L^{\mathrm{PPO}}(\theta_t,z_i) \right\rangle$.

Here $\nabla_\theta f^{\mathrm{return}}(\theta_t)$ is an aggregate return-improving policy-gradient direction.

This is therefore also very close to RFT-Inf:

- local RL attribution compares a sample's PPO gradient with a return-improving target direction inside one PPO round;
- RFT-Inf compares a sample's gradient with the overall RFT training direction and sums this over a surrogate training trajectory.

The difference is mainly the scope and derivation, not the basic gradient-alignment mechanism.

### Relation to influence functions

Influence functions do not sum gradient alignments over the training trajectory. Instead, around a trained local optimum $\hat\theta$, they approximate how removing $z_i$ would move the optimum:

$\Delta\theta_i \approx \frac{1}{N} H^{-1} \nabla_\theta L(\hat\theta,z_i)$.

A target can then be evaluated using

$\Delta f_i \approx \nabla_\theta f(\hat\theta)^\top \Delta\theta_i$.

So influence functions are local around the final optimum, while TracIn and RFT-Inf accumulate first-order effects along a training trajectory.

### Main empirical result

Crucially, on their mathematical-reasoning experiments, RFT-Inf can select only about **20% of the training data** and still outperform RFT trained on the full dataset in the reported settings.

The practical value is therefore not only attribution: the score is used as a data-selection method that substantially reduces the amount of RFT data needed.

# Efficiently Estimating The Influence of Experiences

The useful idea is to associate training data with fixed network masks. In their experiments, each group of 5,000 consecutive transitions always trains through the same mask. They then apply its complementary mask and measure the return change, treating this as an approximation to removing that group's influence. For my research, I could instead assign one mask per source environment and evaluate the complementary policies on a common held-out environment distribution, measuring reward and safety separately. This would require training with masks from the beginning; the paper does not test this environment-level extension.

The evidence supports finding better-performing subnetworks, but does not convincingly establish accurate data attribution. They report no correlation between mask scores and actual leave-one-out effects. Appendix G shows that selecting a PIToD mask and selecting a group for removal through simplified LOO achieve comparable returns in Hopper; it does not show that they identify the same groups. Finding the best mask among many candidates is insufficient to validate the attribution.

The predominantly negative scores in Figure 12 are not inherently invalid, but could reflect disabling useful network components rather than isolating particular experiences: those components also learned from other groups. I would retain the environment-mask idea, but require agreement with leave-environment-out retraining and controls using random masks of equal capacity before trusting it to explain which environments helped learning.

# Understanding Black-box Predictions via Influence Functions

**Classical influence functions** assume training minimizes one fixed objective. Its Hessian, evaluated at the optimum, describes how the model would rebalance after slightly reweighting training data.

**PPO** changes its data and objective across rounds. A Hessian at the final policy describes curvature of whichever objective we choose; it does not automatically capture how earlier experiences affected subsequent learning.

The distinction is **sensitivity of an optimum versus influence through a training history**. Post-training PPO diagnostics can use saved checkpoints and rollouts, but must specify whether the counterfactual holds future experience fixed or regenerates it.
